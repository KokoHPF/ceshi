The efficacy of combined physiotherapeutic 
scoliosis‑specific exercises and manual therapy 
in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis
Zou Wenxia1†, Li Yuelong1†, Zhang Zhou1, Jia Guoqing1, Huang Huanjie1, Zhang Guifang1, Wang Chuhuai1*, 
Lo Wai Leung Ambrose1,2* and Liu Peng1* 
Abstract 
Background  Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a pathological condition characterized by vertebral curvature 
and associated trunk deformities in adolescents. The clinical efficacy of conservative treatment in alleviating spinal 
curvature of AIS remains a topic of ongoing debate. The objective of this study was to investigate the impact of com-
bined physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises (PSSE) and manual therapy (MT) on trunk deformity, spinal func-
tion, mobility, and mental health in patients with AIS.
Methods  Thirty-one participants who were diagnosed with AIS whose Cobb angle was between 10–45°were 
enrolled in the study. Participants in the intervention group received 50 min of PSSE combined with 10 min of MT, 
while the control group performed 50 min of PSSE as their home exercise program. Both treatments were imple-
mented three times a week for four weeks. Cobb angle, spinal mobility, trunk morphology (vertebral rotation angle, 
apical deviation, pelvic obliquity distance and angle), movement capability, and quality of life (QOL) were assessed 
at baseline and post intervention. The treatment effects between the intervention and control groups were analyzed 
using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA.
Results  Following a 4-week treatment period, Cobb angle was significantly reduced from 21.58° to 18.58° in inter-
vention group and increased from 18.00° at baseline and 19.14° post intervention in the control group. Significant 
improvements were also observed in spinal mobility, movement capability, quality of life, and some of the trunk mor-
phology indices in the intervention group compared to baseline (p < 0.05). Improvements were significantly higher 
in the intervention group than the control group.
Conclusion  Combining PSSE and MT shows potential benefits in alleviating AIS symptoms and improving QOL. 
Further studies to substantiate these findings are warranted.
Trial registration  The trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://​www.​chictr.​org.​
cn) with the registration number: ChiCTR2300071357, (Date: 12/05/2023).
Keywords  Scoliosis, Manual therapy, Surface topography, Exercise therapy

Background
The prevalence of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) 
on a global scale has been estimated to range between 2 
and 3% [1]. AIS is a structural spinal disorder that occurs 
during puberty and lacks a definitive etiology. Individuals 
who experience a high severity of lateral curvature dur-
ing their peak growth period may suffer from a decreased 
quality of life (QOL), poor perceived appearance, disabil-
ity, and pain [2, 3]. This condition not only exerts detri-
mental psychosocial impacts, including social barriers, 
elevated suicide rates in individuals with AIS [4], but 
also has a negative psychological effect on the parents or 
guardians [5]. Given the health risks associated with AIS, 
it is essential to identify treatment strategies that mini-
mize the progression of the spinal curve.
To slow down the progression of AIS and correct the 
spinal deformity throughout pubertal growth, non-surgi-
cal treatment such as braces and physiotherapeutic sco-
liosis-specific exercises (PSSE) are recommended as the 
primary approach for mild-to-moderate AIS [6]. Bracing 
is considered an essential therapeutic option for patients 
with AIS who have Cobb angle exceeding 25°. The effec-
tiveness of bracing may be influenced by the manufactur-
ing techniques, brace type, adherence to wearing time 
and patient compliance [7, 8]. PSSE is recommended as 
the preferred treatment for patients with AIS who have 
Cobb angle of less than 25° to slow down the progression 
of the deformity [6]. It focuses on the integration of sen-
sorimotor and enhancement of motor control through 
specific three-dimensional autocorrection and targeted 
exercises [9]. Several studies have suggested that PSSE 
leads to improvements in spinal function, health related 
quality of life, self-image, and psychosocial well-being 
[10, 11]. However, systematic reviews indicate that the 
evidence regarding the effectiveness of PSSE for AIS 
remains inconclusive [12–14].
Manual therapy (MT) can be defined as “the use of 
hands to apply a force with a therapeutic intent”, which 
may include joint mobilization, massage, myofascial 
release, nerve manipulation, strain and acupressure [15]. 
MT of myofascial release and spinal manipulations may 
have the potential to reduce the Cobb angle, prevent sco-
liotic curve progression, improve spinal mobility, and 
alleviate back pain [16]. However, the effectiveness of 
MT in improving Cobb angle of AIS remains inconclu-
sive. Existing evidence suggests that MT may play a role 
in reducing pain, decrease spinal stiffness and restore 
biomechanical dysfunction segments [17]. High-velocity, 
low-amplitude spinal manipulation (HVLA-SM) tech-
niques have been found to elicit muscle spindle activity 
by evoking high-frequency peripheral sensory input [18]. 
Other studies also reported that spinal manipulation 
may facilitate cerebral cortex reorganization to enhance 
top-to-bottom sensorimotor integration and move-
ment control [19]. Post-manipulation observations have 
shown significant increases in neuronal activity, electro-
myographic responses, paraspinal muscle activity, and 
segmental and intersegmental acceleration responses 
[20, 21]. The incorporation of MT may complement the 
benefits of exercise therapy to reduce spinal curvature of 
patients with AIS [22]. To date, there is a limited num-
ber of studies exploring the potential advantages of inte-
grating MT with PSSE, and ongoing research is currently 
being conducted, with results awaiting publication [23]. 
The present study aimed to investigate the effect of com-
bining PSSE with MT in trunk morphology, spinal mobil-
ity, movement abilities and quality of life in patients with 
AIS.
Materials and methods
Recruitment
Participants had been recruited through the outpatient 
service of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen 
University from February 2021 to February 2022. The 
inclusion criteria were that study participants volun-
teered to be subjects of this research, that they had been 
diagnosed with AIS, were aged 10–18  years old at the 
time of the study, had a Cobb angle of 10–45° in tho-
racic or lumbar. Prospective participants were excluded 
from the study where they had non-idiopathic scoliosis, 
exercise contraindications, had previously received treat-
ment for scoliosis, bracing, or could not conduct motor 
training. The trial was retrospectively registered at www.​
chictr.​org.​cn (ChiCTR2300071357), dated 12/05/2023. 
After approval from the institutional review approved by 
the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of The First Affil-
iated Hospital at Sun Yat-sen University (ETHICS No. 
[2021]101) dated 20/01/2021, the data collection pro-
cess was started. Written informed consent was obtained 
from all participants and their legal representatives.
Study design and procedure
All participants were given the options to participate 
in either the intervention group or the control group. 
Standard health education was provided to both groups 
as part of routine clinical care. The participants in 
the intervention group underwent a 4-week interven-
tion comprising 50 min of supervised PSSE and 10 min 
of MT sessions for three times a week. In the control 
group, participants and their parents were taught about 
the PSSE by physiotherapists, and it requires parents to 
record a video recording in the process in 50 min. AIS 
participants in the control group were asked to perform 
50-min home exercise programs three times a week for 
four weeks, under parental supervision with video. The 
benefit of bracing was not investigated in the present

Intervention
Health education
The participants and their parents were provided with 
comprehensive clinical information regarding AIS. This 
information covered the potential adverse effects associ-
ated with AIS, the factors influencing the progression of 
scoliotic curvature, and the importance of seeking suit-
able treatment. Additionally, patients were advised to 
prioritize maintaining proper body posture and incorpo-
rating regular exercise into their daily routines.
Physiotherapeutic scoliosis‑specific exercises
PSSE were delivered by experienced physiotherapists, 
which encompassed a range of techniques including 
spinal stabilizing exercises, breathing training, postural 
correction, and three dimensions autocorrection. The 
intervention program was based on the principles of 
Schroth and Scientific Exercise Approach to Scoliosis 
(SEAS) methods [9, 26, 27]. The levels of exercise diffi-
culty were individualized based on specific clinical needs 
to achieve participants can cooperate with the exercise. 
Empirical evidence has shown that spinal stabilizing 
and core strengthening exercises effectively enhance the 
strength of the musculature surrounding the spine, play-
ing a critical role in mitigating the progression of AIS 
[28]. During the corrective training sessions, patients 
were asked to maintain a neutral or corrective spinal 
position. Breathing training was integrated with the cor-
rective exercises, emphasizing proper inhalation and 
exhalation techniques. It involved drawing air into the 
concave side during inhalation and expelling air from 
the convex side during exhalation, thus rectify the ver-
tebral rotation and thoracic asymmetry. Daily practice 
of postural correction exercises enabled self-recognition 
and self-adjustment, potentially enhancing spinal func-
tion. Yoga balls were utilized to rectify suboptimal pelvic 
positions through rotational adjustments. Participants 
actively engaged in an exercise program involving various 
positions on the yoga mat and yoga balls. Figure 1 illus-
trates examples of PSSE exercises.
Manual therapy
Manual therapy was performed on the intervention 
group, which was prior to the PSSE intervention for each 
section. A three-minute massage was first performed to 
relax the myofascial membrane and muscles surround-
ing the spine. This initial step aimed to alleviate muscle 
tension and minimize the risk of injury associated with 
applying instantaneous pressure to the spine. It also

Assessment
Outcome measures
Cobb angle was the primary outcome measure. Second-
ary outcome measures were spinal mobility, trunk shape, 
movement capability and QOL. All the outcome meas-
ures were assessed at baseline and post intervention. The 
assessment team consisted of three assessors who were 
blinded to the group allocation. Each outcome assessor 
was responsible for specific set of outcome measures. 
Each outcome measure was evaluated twice by the same 
assessor. The two measurements were then averaged to 
obtain the final value for each measure.
Cobb angle
The Cobb angle was determined by measuring the angle 
on the anteroposterior view of the full-spine X-ray image 
[30]. Cobb angle measurement was calculated from radi-
ographs using the UniWeb Viewer (version 8.0, EBM 
Technologies, Taiwan, China) by an experienced phy-
sician. The end vertebrae at the top and bottom of the 
curvature were identified. Lines parallel to the endplates 
of these vertebrae were drawn and extended until they 
intersected. The angle at their intersection, representing 
the Cobb angle, was measured using the angle measure-
ment tool and recorded to assess spinal curvature.
Spinal mobility
Spinal range of motion (ROM) was assessed using a 
handheld inclinometer (MicroFET3, Hoggan Scientific, 
Utah, USA). To measure thoracic flexion, participants 
were asked to stand upright with their feet shoulder-
width apart. They were then asked to flex their thoracic 
region forward, from standing to bending, and place 
the inclinometer on the spinous process of C7 to take 
a reading of the final action. Participants were then 
asked to bend again, and a second reading was taken 
at the T12 spinous process. The difference in the read-
ings provided the ROM of thoracic flexion. Similarly, 
lumbar flexion was measured from T12 to S1. Lumbar 
extension involved an initial reading at T12, followed 
by S1 after maximum extension. Lumbar lateral flexion 
measurements were initiated at T12, with participants 
leaning as far as possible to each side, followed by a sec-
ond reading at S1. All of the procedures for measuring 
aspects of spinal mobility were performed following the 
prescribed guidelines to ensure consistency and stand-
ardization [31].
Trunk shape
Trunk shape was assessed using a surface topography sys-
tem (DIERS Formetric4D, DIERS International GmbH, 
Schlangenbad, Germany) [32]. Participants stood in front 
of the system while maintaining a relaxed stance with 
their backs exposed, particularly the posterior superior 
iliac spine. The system automatically captured, recorded, 
analyzed, and generated various trunk shape indices, 
which show in Table  1. These measurements provided 
objective quantification of trunk shape. The raster-ste-
reographic system is validated extensively against radiol-
ogy, demonstrating high levels of validity and excellent 
intra- and inter-observer reliability in evaluating patients 
with AIS [33, 34].
Movement capability
Movement capability was assessed using the Functional 
Movement Screen (FMS), a tool designed to evaluate 
human movement capability in various settings without 
locomotion [35]. This screening tool comprises of seven 
movements: deep squat, hurdle step, in-line lunge, shoul-
der mobility test, active straight leg raises, trunk stabil-
ity push-up, and rotary stability test. These movements 
assess flexibility, balance, coordination, and neuromus-
cular response abilities. A standardized testing and scor-
ing method were employed, with scores ranging from 1 
(unable to perform) to 3 (correct execution), and a score 
of zero was assigned if participants experienced pain 
while performing a subtest. The cumulative score ranges 
from 0 to 21, representing the total score achieved and 
the higher score means better status.
Quality of life
The Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22) question-
naire was employed to evaluate the QOL in participants 
with scoliosis [36]. The questionnaire measures QOL 
across five dimensions: functional activity, pain, self-
image, psychological state, and treatment satisfaction. 
Each item is scored on a scale of one to five, total score 
for all dimensions is 110 and be computed, with higher 
scores indicating better QOL. The study utilized the Sim-
plified Chinese version of the questionnaire [37].

Statistics
Statistical analysis was conducted in SPSS v.20 (IBM 
SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of the data 
was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Two-
way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to 
assess the effects of groups and different time points. In 
cases where the main effect of time was significant, post 
hoc tests with Bonferroni were performed. Additionally, 
the change rate (obtained by dividing the difference value 
by the baseline value) and change value of each indica-
tor in the two groups before and after treatment were cal-
culated and analyzed using independent samples t-test. 
The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for 
Cobb angle and SRS-22 for spinal deformity patients with 
non-surgical treatments are 5° and 0.11 respectively [38, 
39]. For the FMS, spinal range of motion, trunk shape, 
to detect if changes are clinically meaningful, the MCID 
was obtained by multiplying the standard deviation of 
the baseline scores by 0.2 (i.e., MCID = 0.2 × SD) [40]. The 
statistical significance level was set at P = 0.05.

Discussion
The present study extends the current body of knowledge 
by evaluating the effects of integrating MT and PSSE in 
patients with AIS. It incorporates a range of outcome 
measures to investigate trunk morphology, spinal mobil-
ity, movement abilities, and quality of life. Improvements 
in Cobb angle, QOL, movement capability, spinal mobil-
ity and some of the trunk shape indices were observed 
in participants who received 4 weeks of combined PSSE 
and MT interventions. The change value and change rate 
of the recorded parameters were also larger in the inter-
vention group than the control group, demonstrating the 
potential benefits associated with combining PSSE and 
MT in managing AIS.
Combined MT and PSSE on scoliosis curvature
A significant change in Cobb angle was observed in the 
intervention group (mean Cobb: 21.58° ± 8.15° before 
intervention; 18.58° ± 9.68° after intervention). Although 
the absolute change in Cobb angle did not exceed the 
MCID in the intervention group, the change was consist-
ent with the amount of change induced by PSSE (mean 
decrease of 3.04 degrees) reported in a recent Cochrane 
review [24]. The changes in Cobb angle from the PSSE 
and MT group was also higher than those of home exer-
cise programs alone, which demonstrated a deterioration 
in both Cobb angle and trunk shape indices. This find-
ing indicated the potential benefit of combining PSSE 
and MT in slowing or halting the progression of sco-
liosis compared to home exercise alone. Spinal mobil-
ity, including thoracic and lumbar flexion, lumbar lateral 
flexion, and posterior extension, along with higher FMS 
scores, exceeded the MCID in the intervention group 
but not in the control group. Trunk shape indices includ-
ing spinal rotation angle, distance from the median line 
of vertebral migration, and pelvic tilt, also exceeded the 
calculated MCID. These findings suggest that supervised 
PSSE may improve surface topography characteristics in 
patients with AIS in the short term [41]. The pathogen-
esis of AIS is believed to involve characteristics of muscle 
asymmetry and muscle tension differences between the 
left and right sides of the spine, resulting in spinal imbal-
ance in the sagittal and frontal planes [42, 43]. MT was 
shown to adjust tension along the myofascial chain of the 
spine, promoting overall balance recovery [44], and may 
improve spinal range of motion by adjusting facet joint 
positions [17, 45]. Additionally, decreased muscle spin-
dle activities in AIS patients [46] can be stimulated by 
MT [18, 47]. Reduced muscle strength in AIS is linked to 
higher intervertebral disc pressure, contributing to sco-
liosis [48]. Strengthening core muscles through PSSE may 
reduce intervertebral disc pressure [49] and adjust spinal 
tension integrity, decreasing scoliosis risk [26, 27]. Pos-
tural balance deficits contribute to AIS development [50], 
and PSSE can correct these deficits through exterocep-
tive and proprioceptive stimulation [51, 52].

The control group received PSSE program as home exer-
cises without physiotherapist supervision. The findings 
from the present study did not demonstrate significant 
benefits in reducing or slowing the progress of spinal 
curvature in patients with AIS. PSSE is recommended as

evidence of its effectiveness. However, conflicting results

during 25 months of treatment (p < 0.05 between groups) 
[53]. In a prospective study, Zapata and co-authors noted 
that the difference in Cobb angle between the PSSE 
group and observation group was not significant after

difference in exercise delivery settings. A previously pub-

program, when applied in the clinic under physiothera-

The study by Kuru et al., (2015) reported an increase in 
Cobb angle of 5 degrees and angle of trunk rotation of 
1.5 degrees for the home exercise group [55]. Thus, the 
capacity for each child to learn and perform exercises 
effectively in home setting, potentially coupled with poor 
compliance, may contribute to the negative results of 
PSSE [56, 57].
PSSE has the characteristics of strong feasibility, but the 
specific programs are varied. Many studies have explored 
the efficacy of PSSE combined with other treatment pro-
grams in the treatment of AIS, and the results are mostly 
positive. Schreiber et al. found that PSSE, when used in 
combination with conventional therapy, improved the 
Cobb angle, vertebral rotation, and increased back mus-
cle endurance and body posture in AIS [58]. Rrecaj-Malaj 
et  al. showed that PSSE and Pilates exercises provided 
benefit on the Cobb angle, angle of trunk rotation (ATR), 
chest expansion, trunk flexion and QOL in mild and 
moderate AIS [59].
Quality of life and cosmetic appearance
Spinal curve deformities in patients with AIS can lead 
to both physical and psychological complications [60]. 
The International Society on Scoliosis Orthopedic and 
Rehabilitation Treatment published a consensus report 
emphasizing that the primary goals in treating AIS 
should be deformity correction and aesthetic appearance 
enhancement [6]. Gera et al., found that using a combina-
tion of spine adjustment techniques and PSSE can signifi-
cantly reduce the psychosomatic symptoms in functional 
scoliosis [61]. This finding aligns with the results of our 
study, where the combination of PSSE and MT may 
have better outcomes than home-based PSSE alone. The 
intervention group demonstrated a 4-point increase 
in quality-of-life total scores and exceeded the MCID 
value, whereas the home-training group demonstrated a 
1-point decrease. This finding is consistent with a study 
where home exercise alone was found to have no signifi-
cant effect on QOL [62]. Our findings also shed light on 
cosmetic and physical improvements in the participants 
who received combined MT and PSSE. Participants 
in the intervention group exhibited more significant 
enhancements than participants in the control group in 
the spinal appearance, potentially due to the increased 
symmetry and reduction in spinal curve. This enhance-
ment in appearance corroborates with the observed 
improvement in quality of life, further supporting the 
efficacy of combined intervention of MT and PSSE.

with cautions due to the limitations. The absence of a 
control group, comprising either a general exercise 
group or individual PSSE and MT training groups, 
limits the direct comparisons which hinder the inter-
pretation on the specific impact of each interven-

quantitative support to our positive results. Compli-
ance to PSSE was not monitored in the present study. 
This may lead to incomplete or insufficient treatment in 
both groups. Factors such as high academic pressure or 
inadequate parental supervision are known to influence 
compliance to the prescribed regimen. Center-specific 
factors and the limited representation of patients with 
AIS within the sample may impede the external validity 
of the findings. The sample size of 31 participants may 
be considered small, which affects the generalizability 
of the findings. Given the non-randomized grouping 
in this study, the influence of the families’ choices on 
the experimental outcomes should be considered. The 
four-week intervention period limits the interpreta-
tion of the potential long-term benefits. Future studies 
should incorporate longer follow-up periods to evalu-
ate the long-term benefits of the intervention regimen. 
The inclusion of participants with potentially non-
progressive curves and curvature of less than 20° may 
mask or exaggerate the observed benefit. Observational 
approach may often be adopted for patients with mild 
curvature due to potential of non-progression. How-
ever, this approach is contentious and lacks definitive 
guidance for untreated curves under 20° due to a lack 
of clinical indicators for scoliosis progression [63]. The 
observation approach does not address the patient’s 
anxiety and the stress of the parents, who worry about 
the progression of curvatures [64]. Thus, early identi-
fication and early intervention may be beneficial. The 
SOSORT guideline which indicated that current evi-
dence suggested that conservative treatment was effec-
tive at stopping curve from progression, as well as 
improving the curves at skeletal maturity [6]. Stratified 
sampling was not conducted, and Risser stage and Cobb 
angle size were not applied as influencing factors to 
assess the intervention’s efficacy for different severities 
of scoliosis. Future studies should incorporate stratified 
sampling and consider Risser stage and Cobb angle size 
to rigorously evaluate the efficacy across varying severi-
ties of scoliosis.
Conclusion
The present study provides evidence supporting the 
potential clinical benefits of integrating PSSE with MT 
for managing AIS. The combined intervention approach 
demonstrated improvements in Cobb angle, spinal 
mobility, movement ability, trunk morphology, and qual-
ity of life. However, these findings should be interpreted 
with caution due to study limitations. Larger clinical tri-
als are recommended to substantiate the findings of this 
study and to further explore the long-term benefits of the 
combined approach.
